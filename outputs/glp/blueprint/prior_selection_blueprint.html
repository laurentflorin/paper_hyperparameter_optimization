Marginal Likelihood or Forecast Loss? Choosing BVAR Priors in Real Time

What the prior-selection criterion of Giannone, Lenza and Primiceri (2015) is really optimizing, and when tuning the prior for forecast accuracy does better.

Updated 2026-09-29 07:04 UTC · ladder runs complete: 61 (3 variables), 32 (7 variables), 14 (18 variables)

Evidence so far: 3-variable GLP BVAR (GDP, GDP deflator, fed funds rate) a 7-variable model (adding consumption, investment, hours, real wages) and an 18-variable model (GLP's large list without reserves and the monetary base), 5 lags, US real-time data (ALFRED), 80 quarterly origins 2000Q1–2019Q4, forecasts 1–8 quarters ahead, hyperparameters re-selected at every origin.

The pitch

The marginal likelihood is itself a forecast criterion: the joint, one-step-ahead predictive score over the full sample. Relaxing it one step at a time toward a "GDP RMSE" objective shows that narrow tuning overfits and damages other variables. A joint point-forecast criterion over the last 10–20 years finds a modestly tighter prior that forecasts GDP 10–15% better without hurting inflation or interest-rate forecasts. The marginal likelihood stays best for density forecasts. The gain shrinks with model size: 10–15% with 3 variables, about 5% with 7, and about 2% (not significant) with 18, where GLP's prior is essentially unbeaten. Targeting GDP alone damages other forecasts at every size.

Design

The ladder: from marginal likelihood to targeted RMSE

Under a conjugate prior, log p(y1:T) = Σt log p(yt | y1:t−1). A targeted RMSE objective departs from this in four ways. Each rung relaxes one of them. All rungs share the optimizer (multi-start Nelder–Mead from the marginal-likelihood mode) and the plug-in predictive density, so differences come from the objective only.

  1. Marginal likelihood — full sample, one step ahead, all variables, density (GLP's criterion).
  2. Window — score only the last W quarters instead of the full sample.
  3. Horizon — score the h-step-ahead predictive density instead of one step ahead.
  4. Variables — score GDP alone instead of the joint system.
  5. Target — score the point forecast (RMSE) instead of the density.

Implementation: an exact re-implementation of the GLP posterior (matches covbayesvar to 10−6) with prefix-sum sufficient statistics, vectorized draws with common random numbers, and Rao–Blackwellized h-step predictive densities. Every objective is deterministic and smooth in the hyperparameters.

Finding 1

Dropping the joint system does the damage

Relaxing the window and the horizon barely moves forecasts. Scoring GDP alone is the step that breaks inflation forecasts, and it does not even buy better GDP forecasts. The point-versus-density step adds little on top.

Finding 2

Short windows overfit; 10–20 years finds a better prior

With 12–20 evaluation quarters the GDP-targeted optimum cuts in-window RMSE by a third or more and gains nothing out of sample; the selected prior mostly depends on the optimizer seed. With 40–80 quarters, GDP improves by 11–12%. The joint criterion keeps that gain and leaves inflation untouched. Scoring the full sample back to the 1960s loses the gain again.

Finding 3

Targeting a horizon does not specialize the prior

Each row is a prior tuned for one target horizon; each column is the horizon it is evaluated at. If horizon targeting mattered, the diagonal would stand out. It does not: a prior tuned for 1-quarter-ahead forecasts improves 8-quarter-ahead GDP forecasts about as much as one tuned for 4 quarters. What matters is the loss (joint or GDP-only) and the window, not the horizon.

Finding 4

The price is paid in the densities

Point criteria widen the predictive bands for inflation and the fed funds rate. The joint density criterion (4-quarter log score) over the same windows reproduces GLP's forecasts almost exactly. The cost shrinks as the window grows.

Finding 5 · 7-variable model

With more variables the gain shrinks; the damage from targeting GDP does not

In the 7-variable model (target horizon 4 quarters), forecast-based tuning buys 3–6% on GDP instead of 10–15%. The best criterion is now the joint one-step density score over the last 60 quarters. Targeting GDP alone still costs 19–46% on the deflator, and several of these losses are significant after Holm correction. Joint point tuning helps GDP, consumption and the fed funds rate but costs investment and hours about 5%. Real wages improve under every tuned prior.

Window sweep, 7-variable model

Unlike the 3-variable model, the gains from the joint criteria keep growing up to 80 quarters (20 years). At 80 quarters the joint RMSE criterion improves GDP by 4.7%, the deflator by 7.0% and consumption by 6.2%, at a cost of about 4–5% on investment and hours; the joint one-step density score improves GDP by 5.5% with no cost elsewhere. These give the strongest statistical evidence so far (raw Diebold–Mariano p < 0.01 for GDP at 7–8 quarters) but still do not survive the Holm correction. Scoring the full sample again returns roughly GLP's prior.

Finding 6 · Model size

The larger the model, the less room to beat the marginal likelihood

The same criteria were run on an 18-variable model: GLP's large list without non-borrowed reserves, total reserves and the monetary base, whose post-2008 jumps produced explosive forecasts. In every model the gains grow with the evaluation window, but the ceiling falls as the model grows. With 18 variables GLP's own prior is essentially unbeaten: the joint one-step density score picks it at every window, and the best criterion, the joint RMSE over 80 quarters, gains 2% on GDP without statistical significance. Targeting GDP alone makes GDP itself worse and damages most other variables.

Methodological by-products

  • Vintage consistency. Scoring log levels against a later vintage mostly measures base-year changes (≈20 log points for GDP). Forecasts must be scored as growth from the jump-off.
  • Optimizer choice. Generic Bayesian optimization misses the marginal-likelihood mode by 30–200 log points; the MDD surface is smooth and cheap, so a local optimizer is the right tool.
  • Integrating out the hyperparameters versus plugging in the mode makes no practical difference (±3%).

Inference so far

  • No gain is significant after Holm correction. The joint 60-quarter criterion comes closest: raw Diebold–Mariano p = 0.03–0.05 at every targeted horizon.
  • The Model Confidence Set excludes the GLP benchmark for GDP at 7–8 quarters and keeps the joint 40/60-quarter criteria.
  • Density losses for GDP-only criteria are significant after correction.

Still to do

  • Point criteria at other target horizons for the 7- and 18-variable models (only the 4-quarter target was run).
  • Full GLP large list (with reserves and the monetary base) as a robustness check, or a documented reason to drop it.
  • Pre-register the window (40–80 quarters) in the paper rather than choosing it from these results.
  • Statistical power: 80 overlapping origins give weak tests; consider pooled or panel Diebold–Mariano tests across horizons.

Practical rule for practitioners: if you tune a BVAR prior for forecasting, use a joint loss and at least a decade of evaluation origins; the gain is largest in small models. If you care about densities, or work with a large model, the marginal likelihood is hard to beat.
